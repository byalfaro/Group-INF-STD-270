Our Specialization Dossier

Digital Collections & Imaging Services

Benyamin Alfaro, Paige Lambert, Claire Moylan, Soha Sadeghinejad

Interview

Overview

We interviewed Mario Einaudi, Head of Digital Collections & Imaging Services at The Huntington Library, Art Museum, and Botanical Gardens. Digital Collections & Imaging Services is responsible for the digitization and digital reproduction of materials in Huntington collections for exhibitions and the online catalog to support the institution’s mission of education and public access. Einaudi manages and assists department staff, the Huntington Digital Library, and interdepartmental interactions.

To be successful in digital collections, strong technical skills in metadata, cataloging, digital asset management systems, and content management systems are important. For imaging, a professional photographer skillset and knowledge of standards like FADGI (Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative) are needed. Expertise in these areas ensures an accurate capture of objects for digitization. Additionally, a strong leader in this field understands the institution’s mission, supports and protects staff, takes risks, and advocates for department needs.

Professional development happens as on-the-job training, outside programming, and professional networking. If on-site professional development is not offered, programs like the Northeast Document Conservation Center’s Digital Directions provide opportunities to develop skills for digital projects and project management. However, paying for outside training can be difficult on a librarian salary. For students, internships can be difficult to find. Therefore, working on small projects is one way to learn as much as possible.

Einaudi describes the field as static. Ongoing challenges include funding, unpaid internships, contract positions, a tense political environment, and a lack of understanding about library professions. To combat this, according to him, librarians need to be better about advocating for themselves and teaching people about what happens behind the scenes.

Lastly, while The Huntington does not currently have an AI policy, one is under development. AI is beginning to influence areas, like handwriting transcription and 3D capture, but it cannot replace imaging services, metadata specialists, and catalogers. For these, only humans can capture accurate and descriptive data.

Typical Tasks

A job in digital collections involves using specialized equipment and software to preserve born digital or other reformatted content to make it accessible. Part of the job may require writing and editing descriptive metadata to accompany the digital items in the chosen data asset management system or content management system. Additionally, like at The Huntington, a digital collections department may also provide imaging and digitization services for other departments and the online catalog. Ultimately, the goal is to provide access to materials in collections via digital or online services and ensure long-term digital preservation.

Our Observations:

The upper level roles wear many hats, requiring hard skills related to digitization, DAMs and CMS, and metadata and soft skills related to communication and management. Also, the role title may not specifically state words like “archives” or “digital collections.” The field may instead use terms like “production” or “operations,” as seen in the manager-level position. As a result, expanding the vocabulary used to search for jobs or internships in digital collections may be necessary.

Professional Context

The most common professional organization is Society of American Archivists (SAA). SAA has an internal job board where members can share open positions at their organization. It also offers mentoring opportunities, hosts conferences, and shares the most relevant news for the industry in its own publication. Many professionals who work in digital collections are also members of the American Library Association (ALA). A smaller, but equally important, professional organization is the Digital Library Federation (DLF). Like SAA, DLF hosts conferences; additionally, it has training resources and online chatroom communities. DLF is particularly great for professionals working with digital asset management (DAM). There are also virtual conferences, such as MuseumNext’s Digital Collections Summit, that address topics like AI, collections management, digitization, and online curation in museum contexts. Community outreach is a huge aspect of this field, especially when it comes to sharing positions. Blogs are a great resource for finding internship and job opportunities. In particular, ArchivesGig is a phenomenal resource for those hoping to land a librarian or archivist role. Similar to ArchivesGig, the blog Code4Lib Jobs posts library and archives jobs, including many positions associated with digital collections. The creator of the blog, the organization code4lib, also hosts its own journal, conference, chatrooms, and wiki site. In our expert’s experience, The Huntington did not offer many (if any) professional development opportunities. Employees interested in said development would have to pay for any training out-of-pocket, which can get expensive. That said, The Hungington is not an outlier when it comes to professional development. Whether it is membership to a professional organization or attendance at a conference, few institutions are willing and/or able to (fully) fund these opportunities. In many cases, it is up to each individual to pay their way to participate in these forms of professional development.

Job-Market Outlook

Einaudi mentioned that The Huntington, and presumably other libraries as well, often hire people for short-term contracts. He disagrees with this practice, believing in the value of institutional memory. In this way, he also disagrees with the trend of unpaid internships. Having employees who carry knowledge of how an institution operates is valuable for libraries or archives with large collections, especially a place like The Huntington, which has over 12 million in their collections. Losing staff who bring and take knowledge when they leave is difficult, especially with having to train someone new every time. At the same time, asking someone to forcibly leave due to lack of funding can feel defeating for both the employer and employee. Additionally, Einaudi shared with us his belief that the field is static, and the labor market assessment from Pasadena City College reinforces his claims. Based on the data gathered in the assessment, there was a 2% increase in Los Angeles County for librarianship positions between the years 2020 and 2025 while less dense areas, such as Orange County, had a 0% change. The wages of entry-level positions were also shown to be below the calculated self-sufficiency standard wage in California (AKA a livable wage). So, due to downtrending, there is a relevancy to Einaudi’s idea about the field being static. While job openings remain on a slight increase, it might not be due to the growing career trajectory. Instead, people could be changing fields, seeking upward mobility through higher pay or alternative working conditions. For instance, in our interview, Einaudi shared a story of a bright woman who put in effort to get a senior position in his department, but upper management would not let him promote her. The U.S. Bureau of Labor and Statistics also has findings that support the labor and market assessment and Einaudi’s remarks. They present a job outlook from 2024-2036 estimating a 2% growth, claiming it slower than average than other occupations. While concerning, it is a promising sign that there is still relevancy to librarianship. Parts that are expanding seem to be in the realm of data management, asset management, and digital roles such as librarianship for tech companies.

Technical Skill Snapshot

The main skills needed for jobs involving digital collections and imaging services are cataloging and metadata. Of course, there is more work involved beyond this, like knowledge of how to accurately take images for service use, online digital systems, how to organize different types of files, and editing services for images. Additionally, if in a leadership role, project management skills are a must. Essentially, metadata and cataloging are good basic skills to have. Our expert echoes this sentiment. In the metadata field in 2022 and 2023, there seemed to be a growing number of individuals who retired due to the pandemic, leaving the field open for new hires. For cataloging, in academic institutions at least, according to a study done in 2022, there seems to be a simultaneous grounding in traditional academia and a lifting of requirements for Master’s degrees, allowing these spaces to be filled by a larger applicant pool.

Labor Trends

One common, and unfortunate, trend in GLAMs is the prevalence of temp positions. These limited contracts often do not lead to a permanent position. Due to lack of funding, professionals come on to assist with a task, such as processing a large collection, and are let go once the goal has been achieved. Not only does the lack of stability cause anxiety for the person in the temporary role, it also can cause issues with how the collection is managed. The longer one works with a collection, the better they know its ins and outs, which is beneficial to the organization and its patrons. Generally, funding is a problem in this field. Like many organizations, The Huntington doesn’t particularly use money wisely, and there is a lack of understanding of how things work and how much things actually cost. For example, the board does not understand why the librarians and archivists are asking for more expensive supplies and generally does not approve requests for additional funding. But, having access to these materials would create a better environment for artifacts to be preserved and organized. Additionally, GLAMs often rely on unpaid internships and volunteer work. While these experiences are great from a learning perspective, they are hurdles for those entering the workforce. Most cannot afford to work a position, even with part time hours, without being compensated, especially in HCOL areas. Those preparing to join the GLAM industry should not be afraid to hop around from organization to organization if it means career advancements and better salaries.
